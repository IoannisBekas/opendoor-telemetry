An iBuyer, read cold

Opendoor buys houses with borrowed money and hopes to sell them before the interest eats the margin. Everything below is drawn from public filings and public market data — no paid terminal, no vendor feed.

Where the balance sheet stands

How to read this Opendoor is not a broker taking a fee. It buys the house outright, holds it, and resells it, so its balance sheet is its business. Three numbers govern it: how much property it owns, how much it borrowed to buy that property, and how much room is left to borrow more. Everything else is downstream of those.

What the money costs

How to read this The company borrows through a ladder of separate credit facilities, each with its own ceiling and its own interest rate. Bars show how much of each ceiling is drawn. The figure on the right is the rate that facility charges. Cheap senior paper and expensive mezzanine paper sit in the same stack, so the mix — not the headline rate — sets the true cost of funds.

Borrowing, and the room left to borrow

How to read this Two charts, not one. The first is money: the ceiling across all facilities against the amount actually drawn. The second is the same story as a percentage — utilisation. They are shown separately because stacking a currency scale and a percentage scale on one pair of axes invites the reader to compare two things that share no units.

Inventory against leverage

How to read this Three lines on one currency scale: the property Opendoor owns, the debt secured against it, and the cash it holds free of any lien. When the inventory line and the debt line move together, the company is buying on credit. When they diverge, it is either selling down or funding purchases from its own pocket.

How fast the houses clear

How to read this Opendoor’s risk is concentrated in a handful of Sunbelt metros. Months of supply is the headline measure of how long the local market would take to absorb its listings. Under four months favours sellers; over six favours buyers and squeezes anyone holding stock. Sale-to-list below 0.98 means asking prices have gone stale.
